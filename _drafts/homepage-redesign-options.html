Full about page (long)

~260 words · /about/

I'm a data scientist who has spent twenty years building tools for clinical trials, most of it on one idea: the people responsible for patient safety deserve better than a 200-page PDF.

I got here sideways. I liked building things more than I liked algorithms, taught middle school for a year, then went back to school for biostatistics. I wrote a lot of SAS, discovered R at my first job, and worked as a study statistician on NIH-funded asthma and allergy trials, where I co-authored a stack of papers.

Around 2012 I saw the New York Times' interactive election graphics and asked why clinical trials couldn't have that. My boss gave me two weeks to learn JavaScript. The first chart was terrible and I've been building interactive tools for trial data ever since — eventually building and leading the Graphics Group at Rho, hiring people who still do great open-source work.

That work became {safetyGraphics}: an open-source R/Shiny app for interactive safety review, built with the ASA-DIA safety working group I co-led. For five years, clinicians and data scientists from dozens of companies met, wrote papers, and presented to conferences and to the FDA — because the doctors knew how to monitor trials and we knew how to build the tools.

Since 2021 I've worked on risk-based quality monitoring, where my team builds {gsm}: open-source R packages designed for GxP from the first commit and used to monitor real studies. We co-lead the PHUSE OpenRBQM working group.

Lately the question that won't leave me alone is what agentic AI can actually do in a regulated environment — not the demos, the daily work. I'm giving the R/Pharma 2026 keynote on it this October and publishing the whole build as a developer diary, dead ends included.

Key projects

Six drafted; feature four on the homepage and let the rest live on a /projects/ page. Each has a role line (what you actually did), a one-line definition, and the reason it mattered.

{gsm} — Good Statistical Monitoring

Creator · Team lead · 2021 – present · R

An open-source R framework for risk-based quality monitoring, designed for GxP from the first commit. My team uses it to monitor live studies, and the surrounding tooling — {workr} for workflows, {qcthat} for test and traceability matrices, {gsm.datasim} for simulated data — turns validation evidence into a build artifact instead of a document nobody reads.

gsm.core docs · GitHub · peer-reviewed paper

safety.viz

Creator · 2026 · JavaScript

A charting library for monitoring clinical trial safety. Seven interactive charts — safety histogram, outlier explorer, results over time, shift plot, delta-delta, AE timelines, and the eDISH hepatic explorer — modernized from the original {safetyGraphics} renderers. Every chart traces to a reviewed requirement matrix, with 249 unit tests and 94 browser tests published as audit-style evidence reports: the answer to “is it validated?” is now a link.

Live gallery · GitHub · evidence reports

{safetyGraphics}

Creator · Co-lead, ASA-DIA safety working group · 2017 – 2021 · R, Shiny, D3

The open-source app that replaced the safety appendix with charts a clinician can click. Scattered prototypes — the Adverse Event Explorer, the eDISH hepatic explorer — pulled into one Shiny app for interactive review of a trial's safety data. Built by clinicians and data scientists together across dozens of companies over five years, with papers, conference talks, and a presentation at the FDA.

Website · demo app · hepatic explorer workflow (PDF)

OpenRBQM

Co-lead · PHUSE working group

A cross-company working group building shared open-source standards and tooling for risk-based quality monitoring. The point is the same as it was with safetyGraphics: the hard parts — what to monitor, how to prove the pipeline works — shouldn't be rebuilt in private at every sponsor.

openrbqm.github.io · GitHub

Obot — an agent working in public

Experiment · 2026

An autonomous coding agent on a clean-room laptop, pointed at neglected open-source projects. Thirteen renderers, a local-first app, a billion tokens in one weekend — and a week spent finding out where it had been quietly wrong. Published as interviews with the agent, because the failures are the interesting part for anyone shipping regulated software.
