I'm a data scientist who has spent twenty years building tools for clinical trials,
most of it on one idea: the people responsible for patient safety deserve better than
a 200-page PDF.

Today I'm Senior Director of Biostatistics at [Gilead](https://www.gilead.com/), where
I've been the technical lead for the company-wide risk-based quality management program
since it launched in 2022. My team builds
[`{gsm}`](https://gilead-biostats.github.io/gsm.core/) — open-source R packages for
centralized monitoring analytics that are approved for GxP use and provide monitoring
oversight for more than fifteen active studies. We released `{gsm}` publicly in 2023,
and we co-lead [OpenRBQM](https://openrbqm.github.io/), a pre-competitive PHUSE working
group with members from more than twenty pharma companies, CROs and regulators.

## How I got here

I got here sideways. I liked building things more than I liked algorithms, taught 6th
grade math for a year, and then went back to school for biostatistics. I wrote a lot of
SAS in grad school, discovered R at my first job, and spent years as a study statistician
on NIH-funded asthma and allergy trials with the Inner City Asthma Consortium —
[work I still get cited for](https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=Hn0AnpIAAAAJ),
including the Composite Asthma Severity Index.

Around 2012 I saw the *New York Times*' interactive election graphics and asked why
clinical trials couldn't have that. My boss gave me two weeks to learn JavaScript. The
first chart was the clunkiest interactive graphic ever made, and I've been building
interactive tools for trial data ever since — eventually founding and leading Rho's
Center for Applied Data Visualization, a group of data scientists shipping open-source
visual analytics for biomedical research.

That work became [`{safetyGraphics}`](https://safetygraphics.github.io/safetyGraphics/):
an open-source R/Shiny app for interactive safety review, built by the Interactive Safety
Graphics team I founded and led inside the ASA Biopharm–DIA Safety Working Group. For five
years, clinicians and technical contributors from more than two dozen organizations —
pharma, academia and the FDA — met, wrote papers, and presented our work, including at the
FDA's Innovation Forum in 2019. The doctors knew how to monitor trials; we knew how to
build the tools.

## What I'm working on now

The question that won't leave me alone is what agentic AI can actually do in a regulated
environment — not the demos, the daily work. I'm giving the keynote at
[R/Pharma 2026](/keynote/) this October on building open-source clinical trial tools with
agentic AI, and I'm preparing the whole thing in public as a
[developer diary](/archive.html): experiments, lessons, and honest dead ends. The most
recent results are [safety.viz](https://jwildfire.github.io/safety.viz/), a modernized
charting library for safety monitoring with published test evidence, and
[Obot](/2026/08/20/obot-v4-second-interview.html), an autonomous coding agent I set loose
on neglected open-source projects and then interviewed about the experience.

I was given the [PHUSE Plane Builder Award](https://phuse.global/About_PHUSE/PHUSE_Recognition/Plane_Builder)
in 2025. I hold an MS in Biostatistics and a BS in Mathematics, both from UNC Chapel Hill.

## About the name

*Wildfiring* is a callback to an old website of mine. It seemed like the right name for a
place that collects twenty years of side projects, open-source packages, and half-finished
experiments.
